Eight billion, one doubling at a time

Between 560 BCE and 882 CE the number of people on Earth doubled once — it took 1,442 years. Between 1948 and 1985 it doubled again in 37. Scroll to watch it happen, then take the controls: pick a view, a starting point and a country, and every number on the page tells you where it came from.

Data: Our World in Data, built from HYDE 3.3 (to 1800), Gapminder (1800–1949) and UN World Population Prospects 2024 (1950–2023) · other estimates: US Census Bureau compilation

Seven thousand years in one scroll

Keep scrolling and time moves with you. In the dots view every dot is a million people, dropped at random inside today's borders of the country they lived in; in the density view each country is coloured by how many people share a square kilometre. Drag the globe sideways to turn it. The switch in the corner changes both globes on this page.

Play it yourself

The same globe, now with a play button. Drag it to turn it, hover over a country to see its number. Three settings change what you notice: the view, where the story starts, and the pace.

The pace matters most. With every year taking the same time on screen, the globe stays nearly empty for nine tenths of the run and everything happens in the last seconds. With every doubling of the world's population taking the same time, the distant past flies by because it doubled slowly, and the last century slows down.

    Where the numbers are weak

    Before 1950 every figure is a reconstruction. Most of the world had no census. Historians work back from tax records, farmland, the size of cities and how many people a hectare could feed. For the year 1 CE, published estimates run from 170 to 330 million.

    Before 1700 the series has one figure per century, and before 1 CE one per thousand years. Between them the page assumes steady growth, so plagues and wars shorter than the gap do not show. The Black Death does, because it left the world smaller in 1400 than in 1300.

    The dots show how many people lived in a country, not where inside it: Siberia gets as many dots per square kilometre as Moscow. The page uses the 165 countries with a complete series back to 10,000 BCE; the rest add up to about 75 million people today.
